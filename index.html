01 — The idea

What is a keiretsu?

A keiretsu (系列, roughly "series" or "lineage") is a Japanese form of business network that took shape after the Second World War, when the pre-war zaibatsu conglomerates were broken up. Rather than one family owning everything, independent firms bound themselves together through cross-shareholdings, a shared main bank, interlocking directorships, and the habit of meeting — often a standing presidents' council that gathered on the same day every month, for decades.

Two shapes are usually distinguished. A horizontal keiretsu spans industries around a bank — Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo. A vertical one runs down a supply chain, the way Toyota's suppliers are woven into Toyota. Both trade the speed of a pure market for something a market can't price: members who answer the phone, share what they know, and are still there in a bad year.

The structure has real critics — it can shelter weak firms and shut outsiders out, and Japan loosened many of these ties from the 1990s on. But the underlying instinct travels well, and it's the one this site is built on: a small group of committed peers, meeting on an unmovable rhythm, with obligations that run in every direction.

What we borrow from it

Reciprocity, not transaction
You show up for people who show up for you. Nobody is keeping a ledger, but everybody is keeping count.
A standing rhythm
The meeting happens whether or not anyone feels like it. Cadence is the whole technology.
Small and closed
Four to eight people. Trust scales badly; a circle you can't hold in your head isn't a circle.
Long horizons
Judge it in years, not sessions. The compounding is the point.

A mastermind, specifically

Peers at a comparable altitude who meet on a fixed schedule to work on each other's real problems. Each session gives one or two people the "hot seat": they bring something stuck, the rest ask questions before offering advice, and everyone leaves with a commitment to report back on.

Why scheduling kills them

Most circles die in the first month, and almost never over the idea — they die in the reply-all thread about Tuesdays. Settle the hour once, make it recur, and the group survives long enough to become worth something.

What good looks like

Five people. Ninety minutes, same slot every fortnight. A rotating hot seat, notes somewhere shared, and a standing rule that you tell the group the thing you'd rather not say out loud.
